About Nebius, and why its prices replace VAST.ai

Who they are. Nebius Group (Nasdaq: NBIS) runs an AI cloud: GPU capacity in its own data centres, rented by the hour. It is one of the "neoclouds" supplying hyperscalers, with dedicated-capacity contracts with Microsoft (up to $19.4B through 2031) and Meta (up to about $27B), and it is an Nvidia reference cloud partner.

What a spot price is. Preemptible VMs run on capacity Nebius has spare: it can stop them at any time, with 60 seconds' notice, when it needs the GPUs for a regular VM in that region. The price is set by an algorithm from available capacity and real-time demand, held between a floor and one cent below the on-demand rate. A price near the floor means slack; a price near on-demand means the region is tight.

Why this beats VAST.ai. VAST.ai is a peer-to-peer marketplace of independent hosts, and the old reading was the single cheapest bid among about a dozen offers, taken once a month: one host's asking price on hardware of unknown quality, and a mix of SXM and PCIe cards. Nebius prices one fixed, documented GPU product per region, from an operator that bills it, and refreshes it at least every 15 minutes, with 30 days of history through an open API. That gives a continuous series instead of one point a month, and a price that responds to demand within known bounds.

What it still is not. One provider's price, not a clearing price across a market; it follows Nebius's own fleet and algorithm. Regions differ several times over on the same day, H100 is sold in one region and often sits at its floor, and the API is an alpha version (v1alpha) that could change. Nebius keeps only 30 days, so history before 22 Sep 2026 does not exist.

H100 spot (cheapest Nebius region): $2.15/hr → $0.79/hr (-63% since 23 Sep 2026). For a more robust series, see Epoch AI and SemiAnalysis compute price indices.

What a Token Costs to Make

Kimi-K3 runs dated 7 Aug – 27 Oct 2026 · fetched 28 Oct 2026 · costs captured 28 Oct 2026

What this measures: how many tokens one chip produces for each dollar it costs to own, while every user still sees a set speed. Throughput is measured: SemiAnalysis's open InferenceX benchmark, agentic (multi-turn coding-agent) traces, input plus output tokens. Cost is not: it is SemiAnalysis's own model estimate of the hourly cost of owning each chip at large hyperscaler volume, not a price anyone paid. Tokens per $1 = tokens/s per GPU × 3,600 ÷ $ per chip-hour.

Not a like-for-like sweep. Each chip is benchmarked on its own date, with its own serving framework, precision and concurrency; the table names them. A newer software run can move a chip a long way.

Most of these tokens are cached input. Agentic traces resend long conversation histories, and InferenceX counts every prompt token in throughput. This model's runs have a theoretical cache-hit rate of 95–99%, so a token here is mostly a cached prompt token, which costs far less to serve than a generated one. Set the figure against a blended or cached-input price, not a fresh-input one.

Owning cost, not rent. The cost basis is SemiAnalysis's total cost of ownership per chip-hour for a hyperscaler buying at volume. InferenceX's other published tier, Rent - 3 Year Commit, prices every chip here higher; this card does not use it, so a team renting its chips gets fewer tokens per $1 than shown.

About this chart

What it shows. Peak dense Tensor-FP16/BF16 throughput for each NVIDIA data-centre GPU SKU, plotted against release date. Log scale. Coloured by architecture family (Volta → Ampere → Hopper → Blackwell).

Why it matters. Training FLOPS per chip has grown ~20× in 8 years (125 TFLOPS V100 → 2500 TFLOPS GB300). Combined with cluster scaling, this is what enables each new frontier-model generation.
